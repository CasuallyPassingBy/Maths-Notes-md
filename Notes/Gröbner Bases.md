This note develops tools for computing with ideals in multivariate polynomial rings.

**Def:** A *monomial ordering* is a well ordering '$\ge$' on the set of monomials that satisfy $mm_1 \ge mm_2$ whenever $m_1 \ge m_2$ for monomials $m, m_1, m_2$. Equivalently, a monomial ordering may be specified by defining a well ordering on the $n$-tuples $\alpha = (a_1,\dots, a_n) \in \Bbb Z^n$ of multidegrees of monomials $Ax_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ that satisfies $\alpha+ \gamma \ge \beta+\gamma$ if $\alpha\ge \beta$ .

**Example:** 
- *Lexicographical monomial ordering:* We are considering $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. We first declare an ordering of the variables, for example, $x_1>x_2>\dots>x_n$ and then declaring that the monomial term $Ax_1^{a_1}\dots x_n^{a_n}$ with exponents $(a_1,\dots, a_n)$ has higher order then the monomial term $Bx_1^{b_1}\cdots x_n^{b_n}$ with exponents $(b_1,\dots, b_n)$ if the first component where the $n$-tuples differ has $a_k > b_k$. 

**Def:** Fix a monomial ordering on the polynomial ring $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$.
- The *leading term* of a nonzero polynomial $f(x) \in F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, denoted $\text{LT}(f)$. is the monomial thoerem of maximal order in $f$ and the leading term of $f = 0$ is $0$. Define the *multidegree of $f$*, denoted $\partial (f)$, to be the multidegree of the leading term of $f$.
- If $I$ is an ideal in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, the *ideal of leading terms*, denoted $\text{LT}(I)$, is the ideal generated by the leading terms of all the elements of the ideal $$\text{LT}(I) := (\text{LT}(f) \mid f\in I).$$
**Def:** Let $I$ be an ideal in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. The ideal $\text{LT}(I)$ is by definition generated by monomials. Such ideals are called *monomial ideals* and are typically much easier to work with than generic ideals. 

**Prop:** Let $I$ be a monomial idea generated by monomials $m_1,\dots, m_k$. A polynomial $f\in F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ is in $I$ iff every monomial in $f_i$ of $f$ is a multiple of one of the $m_j$. 

**Prop:** Let $f, g\in F[x_1,\dots, x_n]\setminus\{0\}$. We have the following properties.
- $\partial (fg) = \partial(f) + \partial(g)$
- $\text{LT}(fg) = \text{LT}(f)\text{LT}(g)$. 
- $\partial(f+g) \le \max\{\partial (f),\partial (g)\}$, with equality if $\partial(f) \neq \partial(g)$. 

**Obs:** We can choose $f_1(x, y), f_2(x, y) \in F[x, y]$ with lexicographical ordering $x>y$ such that $I := (f_1, f_2)$, and $(\text{LT}(f_1), \text{LT}(f_2))\subset\text{LT}(I)$. We would like to get a set of generators of an ideal in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ whose leading terms generate the leading terms of all the elements in $I$. 

**Def:** A *Gröbner basis* for an ideal $I$ in the polynomial ring $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ is a finite set of generators $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ for $I$ whose leading terms generate the ideal of all leading terms in $I$, i.e., $$I = (g_1,\dots, g_m) \quad \text{and}\quad \text{LT}(I) =(\text{LT}(g_1), \dots,\text{LT}(g_m)). $$

Note that a Gröbner basis is in fact a set of *generators* for $I$, meaning, that every element in $I$ is linear combination of the generators, not necessarily unique. 

**Cor:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and suppose $\{g_1,\dots, g_n\}$ is a Gröbner basis for the ideal $I$ in $R$. $h\in \text{LT}(I)$ iff $h$ is a sum of monomial terms each divisible by some $\text{LT}(g_i)$, $1\le i \le m$. 

**General Polynomial Division:** Fix a monomial ordering on $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, and suppose $g_1,\dots, g_m$ is a set of nonzero polynomials in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. If $f$ is any polynomial in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, start with a set of quotients $q_1,\dots, q_n$ and a remainder $r$ initially all equal to $0$ and successively test whether the leading therm of the dividend $f$ is divisible by the leading terms of the divisors $g_1, \dots, g_m$ in that order. Then:
- If $\text{LT}(f)$ is divisible by $\text{LT}(g_i)$, say $\text{LT}(f) = a_i\text{LT}g_i$, and $a_i$ to the quotient $q_i$ replce by the dividend $f-a_i g_i$ (a polynomial of lower order leading term), and reiterate the entire process.
- If the leading term of the dividend $f$ is not divisible by any of the leading terms $\text{LT}(g_1), \dots,\text{LT}(g_m)$, add the leading term of $f$ to the remainder $r$, replace $f$ by the dividend $f-\text{LT}(f)$, and reiterate the entire process.
The process terminates when the dividend is $0$ and result in a set of quotients $q_1,\dots, q_m$ and a remainder $r$ with $$f = q_1g_1 +\dots q_m g_m +r.$$Each $q_i g_i$ has multidegree less than or equal to the multidegree of $f$ and the remainder $r$ has the property that no nonzero term in $r$ is divisible by any of the leading terms $\text{LT}(g_1), \dots,\text{LT}(g_m)$. 

**Th:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, and suppose $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ is a Gröbner basis for the nonzero ideal $I$ in $R$: Then
- every polynomial $f\in R$ can be written uniquely in the form $$f = f_I+r,$$where $f_I\in I$ and no nonzero monomial term of the 'remainder' $r$ is divisble by any of the leading terms $\text{LT}(g_1), \dots,\text{LT}(g_m)$. 
- Both $f_I$ and $r$ can be computed by general polynomial division by $g_1,\dots, g_m$ and are independent of the order in which these polynomials are used in the division.
- The remainder $r$ provided a unique representation for the coset of $f$ in the quotient ring $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]/I$. In particular, $f\in I$ iff $r= 0$. 

**Prop:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and let $I$ be a nonzero ideal in $R$.
- If $g_1,\dots, g_m$ are any elements of $I$ such that $\text{LT}(I) =(\text{LT}(g_1), \dots,\text{LT}(g_m))$, then $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ is a Gröbner basis for $I$.
- The ideal $I$ has a Gröbner basis. 

A very natural question is how to test whether a given set of generators of an ideal $I$ is a Gröbner basis, which we then use to provide an algorithm to find a Gröbner basis. 

**Def:** If $f_1, f_2$ are two polynomials in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and $M$ is the monic least common multiple of the terms $\text{LT}(f_1)$ and $\text{LT}(f_2)$ then we can cancel the leading terms by taking the difference $$S(f_1,f_2) := \frac M{\text{LT}(f_1)} f_1- \frac M{\text{LT}(f_2)} f_2.$$
**Lemma:** Suppose $f_1,\dots, f_m \in F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ are polynomials with the same multidegree $\alpha$ and that the linear combination $h = a_1 f_1+ \dots+a_m f_m$ with constants $a_k \in F$ has strictly smaller multidegree. Then $$h = \sum_{k = 2}^m b_k S(f_{k-1}, f_k),$$for some constants $b_k \in F$. 

For a fixed monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and ordered set of polynomials $G = \{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ in $R$, write $f \equiv r \pmod G$ if $r$ is the remainder obtained by general polynomial division of $f\in R$ by $g_1,\dots, g_m$ in that order. 

**Buchberger's Criterion:** Let $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and fix a monomial ordering on $R$. If $I = (g_1,\dots, g_m)$ is a nonzero ideal in $R$, then $G = \{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ a Gröbner basis for $I$ iff $S(g_i, g_j) \equiv 0 \pmod G$ for $1 \le i < j \le m$. 

We can turn Buchberger's Criterion into a algorithm.

**Buchberger's Algorithm:** If $I =(g_1,\dots, g_m)$ and each $S(g_i, g_j)$ leaves a remainder of $0$ when divided by $G = \{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ using general polynomial division then $G$ is a Gröbner basis. Otherwise, $S(g_i, g_j)$ has a nonzero remainder $r$. Increase $G$ by adding the polynomial  $g_{m+1} := r$; $G':= \{g_1,\dots, g_m. g_{m+1}\}$ and begin again. It is not hard to check that this procedure terminates after a finite number of steps in the generating set $G$ that satisfies Buchberger's Criterion, hence is Gröbner basis for $I$. 

**Cor:** Suppose $I$ is a monomial ideal with generators $g_1,\dots, g_n$. Then, by Buchberger's criterion then $\{g_1,\dots, g_n\}$ is a Gröbner basis for $I$. 

**Def:** A Gröbner basis $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ fo $I$ where $\text{LT}(g_i)$ is monic and where $\text{LT}(g_j)$ is not divisible by $\text{LT}(g_i)$ for $i \ne j$ is called a *minimal Gröbner basis*. 

**Prop:** Suppose $I$ is a monomial ideal in $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and suppose $\{m_1,\dots, m_k\}$ is a minimal set of monomials generating $I$, i.e, each $m_i$ is a monomial and no proper subset of $\{m_1,\dots, m_k\}$ generates $I$. Then the $m_i$, $1\le i \le$ are unique. 

**Prop:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. 
- $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ is a minimal Gröbner basis for the ideal $I$ in $R$ iff $\{\text{LT}(g_1),\dots, \text{LT}(g_m)\}$ is a minimal generating set of $\text{LT}(I)$. 
- The leading terms of a minimal Gröbner basis for $I$ are uniquely determined and the number of elements un any two minimal bases for $I$ is the same. 

**Prop:** A minimal Gröbner basis is no unique, but the number of elements and their leading terms are unique. 

**Def:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. A Gröbner basis $\{g_1,\dots, g_m\}$ for the nonzero ideal $I$ in $R$ is called a *reduced Gröbner basis* if
- each $g_i$ has monic leading term, i.e., $\text{LT}(g_i)$ is monic, $i = 1, \dots, m$, and
- no term in $g_j$ is divisible by $\text{LT}(g_i)$ for $j \ne i$. 

**Th:** Fix a monomial ordering on $R = F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. Then there is a unique reduced Gröbner basis for every nonzero ideal $I$ in $R$. 

**Cor:** Let $I$ and $J$ be two ideals in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. Then $I = J$ iff $I$ and $J$ have the same reduced Gröbner basis with respect to any fixed monomial ordering on $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. 

Gröbner bases is very useful in explicitly solving systems of algebraic equations, and is basis by which computer algebra programs attempt to solve systems of equations. 

# Solving Algebraic Equations

Let $S = \{f_1,\dots, f_m\}$ is a collection of polynomials in $n$ variables $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and we are trying find the solutions to the system of equations $f_1 = 0, f_2 = 0, \dots, f_m = 0$. If $(a_1,\dots, a_n)$ is any solution to the system, then every element $f$ of the ideal $I$ generated by $S$ also satisfies $f(a_1,\dots, a_n) = 0$. Furthermore, if $S' = \{g_1,\dots, g_s\}$ is *any* set of generators for the ideal $I$ then the set of solutions to the system $g_1 = 0,\dots, g_s= 0$ is the *same* as the original solution set. 

If the polynomials are linear, then it is trivial, since we can use Gaussian Elimination to solve the problem.

The situation for polynomial equations that are nonlinear is naturally more complicated, but the basic principle is the same.If there is a nonzero polynomial in the ideal $I$ involving only one of the variables, say $p(x_n)$, then the last coordinate $a_n$ is a solution of $p(x_n) = 0$. If there is a polynomial in $I$ involving only $x_{n-1}$ and $x_n$, say $q(x_{n-1}, x_n)$, then the coordinate $a_{n-1}$ would be a solution of $q(x_{n-1}, a_n) = 0$, etc. It we can succesively find polynomials in $I$ that eliminate variables $x_1,x_2, \dots,$ then we will be able to determine all the solutions $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ to our original equations. 

Finding equations that follow from the system of equations in $S$, meaning, finding elements of the ideal $I$ that do not involve some of the variables, is referred to as *elimination theory*. 

**Def:** If $I$ is an ideal in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ then $I_i := I \cap F[x_{i+1},\dots, x_n]$ is called the $i$th *elimination ideal* of $I$ with respecto to the ordering $x_1>\dots>x_n$. 

**Elimination proposition:** Suppose $G = \{g_1,\dots, g_n\}$ is a Gröbner basis for the nonzero ideal $I$ in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$ with respect to the lexicographic monomial ordering $x_1>\dots>x_n$. Then $G \cap F[x_{i+1}, \dots, x_n]$ is a Gröbner basis of the $i$th elimination ideal $I_i := I \cap F[x_{i+1},\dots, x_n]$ of $I$. In particular, $I \cap F[x_{i+1},\dots, x_n] = 0$ iff $G \cap F[x_{i+1}, \dots, x_n] = \varnothing$. 

The study of systems of equations of polynomial equations $f_1= 0, \dots, f_m  = 0$ is intimately related to the study of ideal $I = (f_1,\dots, f_m)$ the polynomials generate in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. This fundamental connection is the starting point for the important and active branch of mathematics called [[Algebraic Geometry]]. 

**Obs:** Suppose $I = (f_1,\dots, f_s)$ and $J = (h_1,\dots, h_t)$, are two ideals in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. Then $I +J = (f_1,\dots, f_s, h_1,\dots, h_t)$ and $IJ =(f_1h_1,\dots, f_ih_j, \dots, f_sh_t)$.

**Prop:** If $I$ and $J$ are two ideal in $F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$, then $tI + (1-t)J$ is an ideal in $F[t, x_1,\dots, x_n]$ and $I \cap J = (tI+(1-t)J)\cap F[x_1,\dots, x_n]$. In particular, $I \cap J$ is the first elimination ideal of $tI+ (1-t) J$ with respect to the ordering $t>x_1>\dots>x_n$.
