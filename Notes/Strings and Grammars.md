We begin a discussion of strings by formally defining a string. To do so, we must introduce the notion of an alphabet and the operation of concatenation. An *alphabet* $V$ is a finite nonempty set of symbols.

The *concatenation* of two alphabetic characters of sequences of characters $x_1\dots x_n$ and $y_1\dots y_m$ is $x_1\dots x_n \circ y_1 \dots y_m := x_1\dots x_n y_1\dots y_m$. 

A *string* over an alphabet $V$ is either a letter from the alphabet $V$ of a sequence of letters derived from concatenation of zero or more characters from the alphabet $V$. With this in mind, we can define recursively as  $$V^1:= V \qquad V^{n+1} := V\circ V^n, \text{for $0< n <\omega$}$$where $A \circ B := \{a\circ b \mid a \in A, b\in B\}$. Then we define $$V^+:= \bigcup_{0<n<\omega} V^n.$$Additionally, we can define $\varepsilon$ as the empty string. We can now define $V^0 := \{\varepsilon\}$, and $$V^* := \bigcup_{n <\omega} V^n = \{\varepsilon \} \cup V^+.$$We can now see the importance of $\varepsilon$ it is the identity of the composition, meaning that for every $x\in V^*$ it satisfies that $x \circ \varepsilon = \varepsilon \circ x = x$. Then we see that $(V^*, \circ, \varepsilon)$ is a monoid. 

Let $x$, $y$ and $z$ be strings over an alphabet where $z = xy$. The string $x$ is a called a *prefix* of *head* of $z$. if $y\neq \varepsilon$, then $x$ is called a *proper prefix* or *proper head*. Similarly, $y$ is called a *suffix* of *tail* of $z$, and if $x \neq \varepsilon$, then $y$ is called a *proper suffix* or *proper tail*.

# Languages and Grammars

**Def:** Let $V$ be a finite alphabet, then a language $L$ is defined as $L \subseteq V^*$. 

**Def:** Let $V_T$ be a finite nonempty set of symbols called the *terminal alphabet*. The symbols in $V_T$ are called *terminal symbols*. The *metalanguage* which is used to generate strings in the language is assumed to contain a set of syntactic classes or variables called *nonterminal symbols*. The set of nonterminal symbols is denoted by $V_N$, and the elements of $V_N$ are used to define the syntax of the language. We assume that $V_N\cap V_T = \varnothing$. The set $V_N \sqcup V_T$ consisting of nonterminal and terminal symbols is called the *vocabulary* of the language. 

**Def:** A *grammar* is define by a $4$-tuple $G := (V_N, V_T, S, \Phi)$ where $V_T$ and $V_N$ are sets of terminal an nonterminal (syntactic class) symbols, respectively. $S$, is a distinguished element of $V_N$ and therefore of the vocabulary, is called the starting symbol. $\Phi$ is a finite nonempty subset of the relation from $(V_T \cup V_N)^* V_N (V_T \cup V_N)^*$ to $(V_T \cup V_N)^*$. In general, an element $(\alpha,\beta)$ is written as $\alpha \to \beta$ and is called the *production rule* or a *rewriting rule*.

**Def:** Let $G = (V_N, V_T, S, \Phi)$ be a grammar. For $\sigma, \psi \in V^*$, $\sigma$ is said to be a *direct derivative* of $\psi$, written $\psi \Rightarrow \sigma$, if there are string $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ (including possibly empty strings) such that $\psi = \phi_1 \alpha \phi_2$ and $\sigma = \phi_1 \beta \phi_2$ and $\alpha \to \beta$ is a production of $G$.

If $\psi \Rightarrow \sigma$, we may also say that $\psi$ directly produces $\sigma$ or $\sigma$ directly reduces to $\psi$. 

**Def:** Let $G = (V_N, V_T, S, \Phi)$ be a grammar. The string $\psi$ produces $\sigma$ ($\sigma$ reduces to $\psi$, or $\sigma$ is the derivative of $\psi$), written as $\psi \stackrel{+}{\Rightarrow}\sigma$, if there are strings $\phi_0, \dots \phi_ n$ with $n >0$ such that $$\psi = \phi_0 \Rightarrow  \phi_1 \Rightarrow \phi _2 \Rightarrow \dots \Rightarrow \phi_n = \sigma.$$The relation $\stackrel{+}{\Rightarrow}$ is the transitive closure of the relation $\Rightarrow$. If we let $n = 0$, we cane define the reflexive transitive closure of $\Rightarrow$ as $$ \psi \stackrel{*}{\Rightarrow} \sigma \iff \psi \stackrel{+}{\Rightarrow} \sigma \quad \lor \quad \psi = \sigma.$$
**Def:** A *sentential form* is any derivative of the unique nonterminal symbol $S$. The language $L$ generated by a grammar $G$ is the set of all sentential forms whose symbols are terminal, i.e., $$L(G) := \{\sigma \mid S \stackrel{*}{\Rightarrow} \sigma \land \sigma \in V_T^*\}.$$Therefore, the language is merely a subset of the set of all terminal strings over $V_T$.

# Classification of Grammars

**Def:** A *context-sensitive grammar* contains only productions of the form $\alpha \to \beta$, where $|\alpha| \le |\beta|$, where $|\alpha|$ denotes the length of $\alpha$. Context-sensitive grammars are said to generated context-sensitive languages.

**Def:** A *context-free grammar* contains only productions of the form $\alpha \to \beta$. where $|\alpha| \le |\beta|$ and $\alpha \in V_N$. Context-free grammars generate context-free languages. 

**Def:** A *regular grammar* contains only productions of the form $\alpha \to \beta$, where $|\alpha| \le |\beta|$, $\alpha \in V_N,$ and $\beta$ has the form $aB$ or $a$, where $a\in V_T$ and $B\in V_N$. The languages generated by such grammars are said to be regular.

Let the unrestricted, context-sensitive, context-free, and regular grammars be denoted by the class symbols $T_0$, $T_1$, $T_2$, and $T_3$, respectively. If $L(T_i)$ represents the class of languages that can be generated by the class of $T_i$ grammars, it can be shown that $$L(T_3) \subset L(T_2) \subset L(T_1)\subset L(T_0),$$Nand therefore the four classes of grammars form a hierarchy.

**Th:** There are context-free languages which are not regular languages.
