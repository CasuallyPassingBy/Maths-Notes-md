# The Energy of Interaction of Two Particles

Most of this notes has been on one particle, now we would like to extend this discussion to systems that have multiple particles. We will first examine systems with only two particles. In this section, we shall suppose that two particles interact via forces $\mathbf F_{12}$, on particle $1$ by particle $2$, and $\mathbf F_{21}$, on particle $2$ by particle $1$, but that there are no other, external, forces. In general, the force $\mathbf F_{12}$ could depende on the positions of both particles, so can be written as $$\mathbf F_{12} = \mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2), $$and by Newton's third law $$\mathbf F_{12} = - \mathbf F_{21}.$$
Any isolated system system must be *translationally invariant*: If we bodily translate the system to a new position, without changing the relative positions of the particles, the interparticle forces should remain the same. Thus we get that $\mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = \mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1 + \mathbf s, \mathbf r_2  +\mathbf s)$ for any vector $\mathbf s$. In other words, $\mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2)$ depends only on $\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2$, since $F_{12}(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) = \mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2, \mathbf 0)$, for any two positions, and we can write $$\mathbf F_{12} = \mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1- \mathbf r_2).$$
This simplifies our discussion. We can learn a lot about the force $\mathbf F_{12}$ by fixing $\mathbf r_2$ at any convenient point, like the origin, which reduces to just $\mathbf F_{12}(\mathbf r_1)$. With $\mathbf r_2$ fixed, our discussion of the force on a single particle now applies. For example, if the force $\mathbf F_{12}$ on particle $1$ is conservative, then it must satisfy $$\nabla_1 \times \mathbf F_{12} = 0,$$where $\nabla_1$ is the differential operator $$\nabla_1 := \frac{\partial }{\partial x_1}\hat{\mathbf x}+ \frac{\partial }{\partial y_1}\hat{\mathbf y} + \frac{\partial }{\partial z_1}\hat{\mathbf z}$$with respect to the coordinates $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ of particle $1$. Given that $\mathbf F_{12}$ is conservative, then we can define potential energy $U(\mathbf r_1)$ such that the force on particle $1$ is $$\mathbf F_{12} = - \nabla_1 U(\mathbf r_1).$$
This gives the force $\mathbf F_{12}$ for the case that particle $2$ is at the origin. To find it for the particle $2$ anywhere else we have only to translate back to an arbitrary position by replacing $\mathbf r_1$ with $\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2$ to give $$\mathbf F_{12} = - \nabla_1 U(\mathbf r_1- \mathbf r_2).$$To find the reaction force $\mathbf F_{21}$, on particle $2$, we only need to invoke Newton's third law, which says that $\mathbf F_{21} = - \mathbf F_{12}.$ That is, we have only to change the sign of the equation above. We can re-express this by noticing that $$\nabla_2 U(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2) = - \nabla_1U(\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2),$$where $\nabla_2$ denotes the gradient with respect to the coordinate of particle $2$. So, instead of changing the sign of the equation of $\mathbf F_{12}$, we can simply replace $\nabla_1$ by $\nabla_2$ to give $$\mathbf F_{21} = -\nabla_2 U(\mathbf r_1- \mathbf r_2). $$
There is a *single* potential energy function $U$, from which we can derive *both* forces. To find the force on particle $1$, we just take the gradient of $U$ with respect of particle $1$; to find the force on particle $2$, we take the gradient with respect to the coordinates of particle $2$. 

Let us consider the conservation of energy for our two-particle system, During a short time $dt$, particle $1$ move through $d \mathbf r_1$ and particle $2$ through $d \mathbf r_2$, and work is done by both particles by the corresponding forces. By the work-KE theorem $$
\begin{align*}
dT_1 &= W_1 = \mathbf F_{12} \cdot d\mathbf r_1 \\
dT_2 &= W_2 = \mathbf F_{21} \cdot d\mathbf r_2 \\
\end{align*}
$$Adding these, we find for the change in the *total* kinetic energy $T = T_1+ T_2$, $$dT = dT_1 + dT_2 + W_\text{tot},$$where $$W_\text{tot} =  F_{12} \cdot d\mathbf r_1 +  F_{21} \cdot d\mathbf r_2 =  F_{12} \cdot d(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2)= [-\nabla_1 U(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2)] \cdot d(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2).$$If we rename $(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2)$ as $\bf r$, then the right side of the equation can be seen to be just the change in potential energy, and we find that $$W_\text{tot} = - \nabla U(\mathbf r) \cdot d\mathbf r = -dU,$$wher the last step follows from a property of the gradient operator. Thus, we get that $$E = T + U = T_1 +T_2 + U, \qquad  d(T+ U ) = 0.$$Note that the total energy of our two particles contains *two* kinetic terms, but only *one* potential term.

## Elastic collisions

Elastic collisions give a simple application of these ideas. An elastic collision is a collision between two particles that interact via a conservative force that goes to $0$ as their separation $\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2$ increases. Since the force goes to $0$ as $\|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2\| \to\infty$, the potential energy $U(\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2)$ approaches a constant, which we may as well take to be zero.

In a collision, the two particles start out far apart, approach one another, and then move apart again. Because the forces are conservative, the total energy is conserved; that is $T+ U =\text{const.}$ But when the particles are far apart, $U$ is zero. Thus, if we use the subscripts $\text{in}$ and $\text{fin}$ to label the situations well before and well after the particles come together, then conservation of energy implies that $$T_\text{in} = T_\text{fin}.$$It is important to remember that there is no principle of conservation of kinetic energy.

# The Energy of a Multiparticle System

The extension of these ideas to an arbitrary number of particles is quite straightforward. For $N$ particles, labelled $\alpha = 1, \dots, N$, the total kinetic energy is just the sum of the $N$ separate kinetic energies $$T:= \sum_{\alpha = 1}^N T_\alpha  = \sum_{\alpha = 1}^N \frac12 m_\alpha v_\alpha ^2. $$
For each pair of particles there is an action-reaction pair of forces; for example, particles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ produce the forces $\mathbf F_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\mathbf F_{\beta\alpha}$. We shall take for granted that each of these interparticle forces $\mathbf F_{\alpha\beta}$ is unaffected by the presence of the other particles and any external bodies. Provided the forces are conservative, we can define a potential energy $$U_{\alpha\beta} = U_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf r_\alpha - \mathbf r_\beta),$$and the corresponding forces are the appropriate gradients $$\mathbf F_{\alpha\beta} = - \nabla _\alpha U_{\alpha\beta}, \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf F_{\beta\alpha} =-\nabla_\beta \ U_{\alpha\beta},$$where $\nabla_\alpha$ represents differentiation with respect to the coordinates of particle $\alpha$.  There are in $(N(N-1))/2$ distinct pair of particles, and for each pair we define a corresponding potential energy. 

For each of the external forces $\mathbf F_\alpha^\text{ext}$ depends only on the corresponding $\mathbf r_\alpha$. Therefore, we can handle $\mathbf F_\alpha^\text{ext}$ exactly as we did the force on a single particle. In particular, if $\mathbf F_\alpha^\text{ext}$ is conservative, we can introduce a potential energy $U_\alpha^\text{ext}(\mathbf r_\alpha)$ and the corresponding force is given by $$\mathbf F_\alpha^\text{ext} = \nabla_\alpha U_\alpha^\text
{ext}(\mathbf r_\alpha)$$

We can now put all the potential energies together and define the total potential energy as the sum $$U := U^\text{int}+ U^\text{ext} = \sum_{1 \le \alpha < \beta \le N}U_{\alpha\beta} + \sum_{\alpha = 1}^N U_\alpha^\text{ext}.$$From this we can prove that in general $$-\nabla_\alpha U = (\text{net force on particle }\alpha).$$
The second crucial property of potential energy $U$ is that (given all the forces concerned are conservative), the total energy, defined as $E = T +  U$, is conserved.
