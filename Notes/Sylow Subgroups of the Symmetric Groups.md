If $G = S_p$, with $p$ prime, then we know that $|S_p| = p (p-1)!$, and $p \not\mid (p-1)!$, thus the Sylow $p$-groups of $S_p$ must have order exactly $p$. Thus, the Sylow $p$-groups must be cyclic, and $S_p$ has exactly $(p-1)!$ number of $p$-cycles.  If $n_p$ is the number of Sylow $p$-groups, then $n_p (p-1) = (p-1)!$, and $n_p = (p-2)!$. Lastly, by Sylow's third theorem, we know that $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$, and $(p-2)! \equiv 1 \pmod p$. Getting another proof of [[Wilson's Theorem]].

## Sylow Subgroups of $S_n$

Given an prime $p$, we consider the regular regular $p$-gon, which we will denote by $P_1$. We use a line segment to represent when $p = 2$. 

We know consider $p$ smaller copies of $P_1$, and we put them at the vertices of $P_1$ such that there's no overlap. We call $P_1$ to this collection of $P_1$'s. Now, we consider $p$ smaller copies of $P_2$ placed at the vertices of $P_2$ such that there's no overlap. We call this new figure $P_3$. 

We can iterate this construction of the polygon $P_t$, that is given by $p^{t-1}$ copies of $P_1$. We are interested in subgroup of symmetries of $P_t$ by rotations that leave it invariant. Let us denote this group by $K_t$. The way $K_t$ acts on $P_t$ it is as follows: there are $p$ copies of $K_{t-1}$ that acts on each copy of $P_{t-1}$ that is at each vertex, and we can rotate the whole structure by $2\pi/p$ radians. 

Let us observe that $K_1 \cong C_p$. 

Let $R_k$, with $1 \le k \le p$, be the subgroup of $K_t$ formed by the rotations of $k$-th copy of $P_{t-1}$ inside $P_t$. We consider the following product $R_1\times R_2 \times\dots \times R_p \subseteq K_t$. Let $t$ be the rotation of all $P_t$ by $2\pi /p$ radians. We tag each $P_{t-1}$ inside $P_t$ beginning at the top and going clockwise by $(1, 2,\dots, p)$. We know consider the composition $t(r_1, \dots, r_p) t^{-1}$: $$(1, 2,\dots, p) \stackrel{t^{-1}}{\longrightarrow} (2,\dots,p, 1)\stackrel{(r_1,\dots, r_p)}{\longrightarrow} (r_1(2), \dots, r_{p-1}(p), r_p(1))\stackrel{t}{\longrightarrow}(r_p(1), r_1(2),\dots, r_{p-1}(p))$$
Thus $t(r_1,\dots, r_p) t^{-1} = (r_p, r_1, \dots, r_{p-1})$ Thus, $t(R_1\times \dots \times R_p) t^{-1} = R_1\times \dots \times R_p$.

**Prop:**
- $K_t = (R_1\times \dots\times R_p)\langle t\rangle$, where $\langle t \rangle$ is the cyclic subgroup generated by $t$.
- $(R_1\times \dots \times R_p) \cap \langle t\rangle = 1$.
- $R_1\times \dots \times R_p \triangleleft K_t$.
Thus, $K_t$ is the semidirect product of $R_1\times \dots \times R_p$ with $\langle t \rangle$.

Now we have seen that $K_t = (R_1\times \dots \times R_p) \rtimes \langle t\rangle$, we can see that $R_k \cong K_{t-1}$, and $R_1\times \dots \times R_p\cong K_{t-1}^p$. We can write the decomposition of $K_t$ as $$K_t \cong K_{t-1}\wr \langle t\rangle.$$
Since we $K_1 \cong C_p$, and $\langle t \rangle$, then $$K_2 \cong K_1 \wr C_p\cong C_p \wr C_p,$$and $$K_3 \cong K_2 \wr C_p \cong (C_p \wr C_p) \wr C_p.$$We can use this recursively, and $$K_t = (C_p \wr C_p \dots \wr C_p) \wr C_p.$$
With these actually gives us that $$|K_t| = p^{p^{t-1}+p^{t-2}+\dots + p +1}.$$We can actually see this order is the correct order of the Sylow $p$-subgroup of $S_{p^t}$. Thus, $$(C_p \wr \dots \wr C_p) \wr C_p$$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $S_{p^t}$. 

We can use this last to calculate a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $S_n$. We write $n$ is [[Different Basis Representation for Numbers|base]] $p$ $$n = a_0 + a_1p +\dots a_r p^r  =(a_r\cdots a_1a_0)_p .$$
We know split the set $\{1,\dots, n \}$ as $a_0$ subsets of $X_0$ with $1 = p^0$ elements, $a_1$ subsets of $X_1$ with $p$ elements, $a_2$ elements $X_2$ with $p^2$ elements, and so on. Each of the subsets $X_k$ has $p^k$ elements, and so $S_{X_k} \cong S_{p^k}$. We can construct a Sylow $p$-subgroup pf $H_k$ of $S_{p^k}$. Finally, $H_0^{a_0} \times H_1^{a_1} \times \dots \times H_{r}^{a_r}$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of the product $$\prod_{i = 0}^r S_{p^i}^{a_i}\cong S_n.$$
