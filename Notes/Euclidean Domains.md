All the rings in this note are commutative

**Def:** Any function $N: R \to \Bbb N$ with $N(0) =$ is called a norm on the integral domain $R$. If $N(a)> 0$ for $a\ne 0$ define $N$ to be a *positive norm*. 

**Def:** The integral domain $R$ is said to be an *Euclidean Domain*, or posses *Divsion Algorithm,* if there is a norm on $R$ such that any two elements $a$ and $b$ of $R$ with $b \ne 0$ there exists elements $q$ and $r$ in $R$ with $$a = bq +r\quad \text{with $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$}. $$The element $q$ is called a *quotient* and the element $r$ is the *remainder* of the division. 

The importance of the existence of a Division Algorithm on an integral domain $R$ is that allows a *[[Euclidean Algorithm]]* for two elements of $a$ and $b$ of $R$: by successive 'division' we can write $$\begin{align*} a&= q_0 b+r_0 \\ b &= q_1 r_0+ r_1 \\ r_0 &= q_2 r_1 + r_2 \\ &\vdots \\ r_{n-2} &= q_nr_{n-1} + r_n \\ r_{n-1} &= q_{n+1} r_n\end{align*}$$
where $r_n$ is the last nonzero remainder. Such an $r_n$ exists since $N(b) > N(r_0) > N(r_1) >\dots > N(r_n)$ is a decreasing sequence of nonnegative integers if the remainder are nonzero, and such sequence cannot continue indefinitely. Note also that there is no guarantee that these elements are *unique*. 

**Examples:**
- Fields are trivial examples of Euclidean Domains.
- The integers $\Bbb Z$ are a Euclidean Domain with norm given $N(a) = |a|$, the usual absolute value. 
- If $F$ is a field, then the polynomial ring $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain with norm given by $N(p(x)) = \deg(p(x))$. 
- The [[Quadratic Integers|quadratic integer]] rings $\cal O$ are integral integral domains with a normed defined by the absolute value of the field norm, but in general $\cal O$ is not Euclidean with respect to this norm, or any other norm. For the cases where $D= -1, -2, -3, -7, -11$, we get that $\Bbb Z[\omega(D)]$ is an Euclidean domain. 
- A [[Discrete Valuation Rings|discrete valuation ring]] is easily seen to be a Euclidean Domain with respect to the normbed defined by $N(0) = 0$ and $N=\nu$ on the nonzero elements of $R$. 

**Prop:** Every ideal in an Euclidean domain is principal. More precisely, if $I$ is any nonzero ideal in the Euclidean Domain $R$ then $I = (d)$, where $d$ is any element of $I$ of minimum norm.

**Cor:** Every Euclidean Domain is a [[Principal Ideal Domain|principal Ideal domain]]. 

**Def:** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and let $a, b\in R$ with $b\ne 0$.
- $a$ is said to be a *multiple* of $b$ if there exists an element $x\in R$ with $a = bx$. In this case $b$ is said to *divide* $a$ or be a *divisor* of $a$, written $b\mid a$.
- A *greatest common divisor* of $a$ and $b$ is nonzero element $d$ such that:
	- $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$
	- if $d'\mid a$ and $d'\mid b$, then $d'\mid d$
	The greastest common divisor of $a$ and $b$ will be denoted by $\text{gcd}(a,b)$, or by abusing notation, simple $(a,b)$. 

**Obs:** Note that $b\mid a$ in a ring $R$ iff $a\in (b)$ iff $(a)\subseteq (b)$. In particular, if $d$ is any divisor of both $a$ and $b$, then $(d)$ must contain both $a$ and $b$ and hence contain the ideal generated by $a$ and $b$. If $I$ is an ideal of $R$ generated by $a$ and $b$, then $d$ is a greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$ if
- $I$ is contained in the principal ideal $(d)$, and
- If $(d')$ is any principal ideal containing $I$, then $(d) \subseteq (d')$. 

**Prop:** If $a$ and $b$ are nonzero elements in the commutative ring $R$ such that the ideal generated by $a$ and $b$ is a principal ideal $(d)$, then $d$ is the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$. 

**Prop:** Let $R$ be an integral domain. If two elements of $d$ and $d'$ of $R$ generate the same principal ideal, then $d' = ud$ for some unit $u$ in $R$. In particular, if $d$ and $d'$ are both greatest state common divisors of $a$ and $b$, then $d' = ud$ for some unit $u$.
